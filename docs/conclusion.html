Conclusion

Summary of Findings

Across five weeks of analysis, the computational evidence consistently supports the central claim of this portfolio: Minimal Tech House and Hard House are measurably distinct genres, not just different labels for similar music.

At the corpus level (Week 8), the two genres occupy clearly different regions of Spotify’s feature space. Energy, tempo, and loudness all separate them cleanly, and the chroma analysis of East Bridge Romance showed that even within a single Hard House track, form emerges from textural contrast around a narrow harmonic core rather than from harmonic complexity.

Looking at individual track structure (Week 9), the self-similarity matrices revealed that chroma and timbre capture fundamentally different dimensions. A track can repeat the same chords throughout while still evolving texturally, a distinction that would be invisible in a purely harmonic analysis. The keygram analysis (Week 10) reinforced this: EDM tracks like Wood maintain a stable key with no modulation, reflecting a compositional philosophy built on repetition rather than development.

At the rhythmic level (Week 11), Hard House maintains a rigid, unwavering beat, while Minimal Tech House distributes its rhythmic energy more diffusely. The tempograms and novelty functions make this contrast visually and numerically concrete.

The classification and clustering analysis (Week 12) tied everything together. A random forest classifier achieved approximately 99.5% accuracy with 10-fold cross-validation, and a reduced model using only five features performed equally well. Hierarchical clustering recovered the genre boundary without any labels, validating the supervised results through an independent, unsupervised approach.

The three features with the most discriminatory power are Tempo (120–128 vs 138–150 BPM), Loudness (−10 dB vs −6 dB), and Energy (sustained groove vs explosive intensity).

Who Could Benefit

These findings are relevant for several audiences. DJs and curators could apply similar feature-based approaches to sort large music libraries, identify tracks that bridge genres, or discover unexpected overlaps between playlists. Music producers gain concrete insight into which measurable properties define genre identity, helping them position their tracks within or across genre boundaries. Researchers in Music Information Retrieval can see how standard Spotify features perform on a focused, two-genre classification task, and importantly, where those features fall short. Spotify’s features say nothing about arrangement, mixing technique, sound design, or cultural context, all of which are central to how listeners and practitioners actually experience these genres.

What I Learned

Working through this portfolio taught me to approach music analytically without losing sight of what makes it meaningful as a listener and DJ. The course introduced standard MIR tools (chroma features, MFCCs, key estimation, classification) in the context of well-known examples, but applying them to my own corpus required adapting each method to a domain where the differences are more subtle than, say, comparing classical music to pop.

One example of this is the reduced-model experiment in Week 12. The course covered random forests and feature importance as general tools, but I independently decided to test whether a minimal subset of features could match the full model’s accuracy. The result (five features performing as well as ten) was not something the course explicitly demonstrated, and it gave me a concrete answer to the question of which features actually matter for this genre pair.

Similarly, combining supervised and unsupervised methods on the same data was my own decision. The course taught each technique separately, but applying both to the same corpus and comparing the results (the classifier’s near-perfect accuracy and the dendrogram’s clean two-branch structure) provided a form of converging evidence that neither method could offer alone. This kind of methodological triangulation is something I now see as essential for drawing credible conclusions from computational analyses.

Limitations & Future Work

This analysis relies entirely on Spotify’s proprietary audio features and on chroma and timbre exports from Sonic Visualiser. Both are approximations. Spotify’s features are black-box estimates that may not correspond to perceptual or musicological categories, and the Sonic Visualiser exports depend on algorithm-specific assumptions about tuning, windowing, and spectral resolution.

The corpus itself is limited to two curated playlists, meaning that the results reflect these particular selections rather than the genres as a whole. A track that appears in one playlist but not another might change the analysis. Future work could expand the corpus to include multiple playlists from different curators, incorporate additional analytical methods like structural segmentation or harmonic rhythm analysis, or apply the same methodology to genre pairs where the distinction is less clearcut.

Despite these limitations, the consistency of results across five independent analysis methods (feature distributions, self-similarity, tonal analysis, temporal analysis, and machine learning) provides strong converging evidence. What the body feels on the dance floor, the difference between swaying and jumping, has a computational signature. The machine can hear it too.
